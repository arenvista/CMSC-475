## Multihead Attention

If the attention matrix $P$ is diagonal, then each word can only attend to itself — meaning all context is ignored.
This can occur with a single attention head; the solution is to use multiple attention heads, each with different learned projections.

For $n$ heads:
$$
\begin{gathered}
Z_1 := \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q_1 K_1^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_1 \\
Z_2 := \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q_2 K_2^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_2 \\
\vdots \\
Z_n := \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q_n K_n^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_n \\
\end{gathered}
$$

The outputs are concatenated and then projected:
$$ Z_{\text{Final}} := W_o [Z_1; Z_2; \dots; Z_n] $$ 
where $W_o$ is a learned linear projection.

Here, $d_k$ is the dimensionality of the key vectors $K_i$ for each attention head. It is typically chosen as $(\text{model dimension})/n$, where $n$ is the number of heads. This ensures that the concatenated output from all heads has the same dimension as the model embedding size.

Recall: The decoder should generate a vector which generates the next word from the context of the previous words in the input stream.

## Cross-Attention: Bridging the Encoder and Decoder

In a standard sequence-to-sequence Transformer (like a translation model), the Decoder's job is to generate the target sequence. To do this accurately, the **Decoder must "talk" to the Encoder**. 

Cross-Attention is the specific mechanism that allows the Decoder to look back at the original source sentence and decide which translated words align with which source words.

### 1. The High-Level Flow
The translation process is split into two continuous phases:

1.  **The Encoder:** Processes the entire source sequence $X$ (e.g., "Me encanta UMBC") all at once into a rich, context-aware memory matrix $Z_{\text{enc}}$.
2.  **The Decoder:** Operates autoregressively. It takes the target words generated so far, combines them with the Encoder's memory $Z_{\text{enc}}$, and predicts the next token.

$$
\text{Encoder}(X) \rightarrow Z_{\text{enc}}
$$
$$
\text{Decoder}(Z_{\text{enc}} + [\text{Generated Context}]) \rightarrow \text{Next Token}
$$

**Autoregressive Generation Table:**
The table below illustrates how the Decoder uses the context of what it has already generated (alongside the Encoder's memory) to predict the next word step-by-step.

| Step | Decoder Input (Context) | Model Prediction (Next Token) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | `[START]` | "I" |
| 2 | `[START]` "I" | "love" |
| 3 | `[START]` "I love" | "UMBC" |
| 4 | `[START]` "I love UMBC" | `[STOP]` |

*(Note: If the model predicts an incorrect token at Step 1, such as Case 2: "UMBC", the subsequent autoregressive steps will pivot to generate text based on that flawed context. This is why Teacher Forcing is used during training).*

---

### 2. The Decoder Block Pipeline

To successfully generate the next token, the input passes through a specific pipeline within each Decoder block. Your notes accurately captured the major steps, but the standard architecture includes one additional Add & Norm step after the Feed-Forward layer. 

The complete flow is:

$$
\text{Masked Self-Attention} \rightarrow \text{Add \& Norm} \rightarrow \textbf{Cross-Attention} \rightarrow \text{Add \& Norm} \rightarrow \text{FFN} \rightarrow \text{Add \& Norm}
$$

**Breaking down the steps:**
1.  **Masked Self-Attention:** The Decoder looks at the English words it has generated so far. It is "masked" so it cannot look ahead at future words (which it hasn't generated yet).
2.  **Cross-Attention:** The Decoder takes its current understanding of the English sentence and compares it against the Encoder's memory of the Spanish sentence.
3.  **Feed-Forward Network (FFN):** A fully connected layer (your $\mathbb{F}$) that processes the combined attention output to finalize the representation before passing it to the next block or the final output layer.

---

### 3. The Math of Cross-Attention

In standard *Self-Attention*, the Queries ($Q$), Keys ($K$), and Values ($V$) are all derived from the exact same input sequence. 

In **Cross-Attention**, the sources are split. This is how the "communication" mathematically occurs:

* **Queries ($Q$) come from the Decoder:** The Decoder takes the output of its Masked Self-Attention layer and asks, *"Based on the English words I have generated so far, what information do I need next?"*
* **Keys ($K$) and Values ($V$) come from the Encoder:** The Encoder provides its final memory matrix $Z_{\text{enc}}$. The Keys represent the "tags" or "labels" of the Spanish words, and the Values represent their actual contextual meanings.

Using learnable weight matrices ($W^Q, W^K, W^V$), we project these inputs:

$$
\begin{aligned}
    Q &= H_{\text{dec}} W^Q \quad \text{(Decoder's current state)} \\
    K &= Z_{\text{enc}} W^K \quad \text{(Encoder's memory)} \\
    V &= Z_{\text{enc}} W^V \quad \text{(Encoder's memory)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we apply the standard attention formula. We find the dot product of the Decoder's Queries and the Encoder's Keys, scale it by $\sqrt{d_k}$, apply Softmax to get the alignment probabilities, and multiply by the Encoder's Values:

$$
\text{CrossAttention}(Q, K, V) = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

The output of this equation is a matrix that seamlessly blends the grammatical structure of the target language (Decoder) with the semantic meaning of the source language (Encoder).
