## Translation

Consider a model designed to translate from Spanish to English.

* $f(x) := \text{Transformer}$
* $x := \text{"Me encanta UMBC"}$
* $y := \text{"I love UMBC"}$

Transformers are akin to an Encoder-Decoder architecture:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 
    &  & \\ 
    & \text{Encoder Matrix } E &  \\ 
    \\ 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \\ \vec{x} \\ \\ \end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix} \\ \vec{z} \\ \\ \end{bmatrix}
\quad \quad
\begin{bmatrix}
    &  & \\ 
    & \text{Decoder Matrix } D & \\ 
    \\ 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \\ \vec{z} \\ \\ \end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix} \\ \hat{\vec{x}} \\ \\ \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$E \vec{x} = \vec{z} \quad \quad D \vec{z} = \hat{\vec{x}} \approx \vec{x}$$

*(Note: As mentioned previously, for translation, the decoder's output is typically aiming for $\hat{\vec{y}} \approx \vec{y}$, while the math above perfectly describes an autoencoder's reconstruction).*

## Positional Encoding

Because Transformers process all tokens simultaneously, we must inject information about the sequence order.

$$
\begin{aligned}
    PE(pos, 2i)   &= \sin\left( \frac{pos}{10^{4 \cdot \frac{2i}{d}}} \right) \\
    PE(pos, 2i+1) &= \cos\left( \frac{pos}{10^{4 \cdot \frac{2i}{d}}} \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Variables:**
* $pos$: Position of the token in the input sequence.
* $d$: Dimensionality of the vector.
* $i$: Index over the vector dimensions. (e.g., If $d = 512 \implies i \in [0, 255]$).
* $10^4$: Represents an arbitrarily large base scaling factor.

**Wavelengths (Geometric progression from $2\pi$ to $2 \cdot 10^4 \pi$):**
* **Smaller $i$:** Smaller wavelength $\implies$ **more** oscillations.
* **Larger $i$:** Longer wavelength $\implies$ **fewer** oscillations.

> *"At the beginning of the vector ($i$ is small), there are more oscillations. At the end of the vector ($i$ is large), the wavelength is longer, and thus there are fewer oscillations."*

## Encoder Details

A naive approach passes each word independently through a Fully Connected Layer (FCL):

$$
\begin{gathered}
[x_1] \xrightarrow{\text{FCL}} [z_1] \\ 
[x_2] \xrightarrow{\text{FCL}} [z_2] \\ 
[x_3] \xrightarrow{\text{FCL}} [z_3] 
\end{gathered}
$$

**The problem:** This lacks context. Words are processed in isolation. 
**The solution:** We construct an **Attention Layer** to blend context across tokens.

## Attention Layer

$$
[ \vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \vec{x}_3 ]
\begin{bmatrix} \\ & \text{ Self Attention Layer/Matrix } & \\ \\ \end{bmatrix} = 
[ \vec{z}_1, \vec{z}_2, \vec{z}_3 ]
$$

Let $P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be an attention weight matrix, where each entry indicates the "importance" or relationship between word $x_i$ and word $x_j$.

$$
P = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    p(x_1,x_1) & p(x_1,x_2) & p(x_1,x_3) \\
    p(x_2,x_1) & p(x_2,x_2) & p(x_2,x_3) \\
    p(x_3,x_1) & p(x_3,x_2) & p(x_3,x_3)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

To apply this, we project the inputs into "values" ($v$) using a weight matrix $W$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
    v_1 &= x_1 W \\
    v_2 &= x_2 W \\
    v_3 &= x_3 W
\end{aligned}
$$

We then use the matrix $P$ to calculate the final context-aware output ($z$). For the first token $z_1$, it is the sum of all values weighted by their relationship to $x_1$:

$$z_1 = P(\text{row 1}) \cdot v$$

As a complete matrix operation:
$$W x = v \quad \quad P v = z$$
$$P W x = z$$

##### Meaning of K and Q
In the context of Transformers and the Attention Mechanism, **$w^k$ and $w^q$ are learnable weight matrices.** Their job is to project your original input words (or tokens) into a new mathematical space where the model can calculate how much each word should "pay attention" to every other word. 

Because neural networks learn by adjusting weights, $w^k$ and $w^q$ are the actual parameters the Transformer updates during training to get better at understanding language.

Here is the breakdown of how they work conceptually and mathematically.

### The Intuition: The "Search Engine" Analogy
To understand *why* we need two different matrices, it helps to think of the Attention mechanism like a retrieval system (like a database or a search engine).

* **Query ($q$): "What am I looking for?"** When the model is currently processing a specific word, that word acts as a *Query*. It is essentially asking the rest of the sentence, "Does anyone have information relevant to me?"
* **Key ($k$): "What do I have to offer?"**
    At the same time, every word in the sentence also acts as a *Key*. It holds up a tag describing the information it contains. 
* **Similarity (The Dot Product):**
    If a word's Query strongly matches another word's Key, they have high similarity. The model decides that these two words are highly relevant to one another and "pays attention" to the match.

### 2. The Mathematical Role
Your notes perfectly capture the first step of this process. You cannot just compare the raw input word embeddings ($x_1, x_2$, etc.) directly to each other because a word might mean something completely different when it is the *subject* of a sentence versus when it is the *context* for another word.

Instead, we multiply the input $x_1$ by our two distinct weight matrices to create two distinct vectors for the exact same word:

$$x_1 w^q = q_1 \quad \text{(The Query vector for word 1)}$$
$$x_1 w^k = k_1 \quad \text{(The Key vector for word 1)}$$

Even though $q_1$ and $k_1$ both come from $x_1$, they represent completely different things. $q_1$ represents what $x_1$ is searching for, and $k_1$ represents what $x_1$ contains.

### 3. A Gentle Correction on the Matrix Math
Your notes summarize the conclusion beautifully: we multiply the Queries and the Keys together to get an attention matrix ($P$). However, to make it technically precise to how the Transformer architecture calculates this, there are two small additions to be aware of:

**A. The Transpose**
To calculate the similarity (dot product) between all Queries and all Keys at once using matrices, we actually multiply the Query matrix $Q$ by the **transpose** of the Key matrix $K^T$:
$$Q K^T = \text{Similarity Scores}$$

**B. The Softmax function**
Right now, $QK^T$ just gives us raw, unbounded similarity numbers. Your notes mention converting this to a **probability** (where everything adds up to 1, and no numbers are negative). To do this, we wrap the calculation in a **Softmax** function (and usually scale it down by a constant factor so the numbers don't get too massive):

$$P = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)$$

*(Where $d_k$ is just the dimension size of the key vectors).* Once you have $P$, you have successfully mapped out the "importance" relationship of every word to every other word!
